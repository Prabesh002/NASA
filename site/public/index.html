Rasuwa, Nepal. 26 August 2026

The Rasuwa flood, traced from space by radar

On 26 August 2026 a flood came down this valley in Rasuwa. A NASA radar satellite had passed over a week earlier and came back five days after. I compared the two passes, and the map is coloured wherever the ground looked different the second time.

Scroll to follow the flood downstream, or use the Next button at the top. You can click the map anywhere to see the radar record of that spot.

The source, north of Langtang Lirung

ICIMOD reports that a slab of rock and glacier ice broke off the north face of Langtang Lirung and fell about 1,200 m into the valley below. This is where the radar change begins and where it is strongest.

Sources

Down the Lende Khola

The debris ran down the Lende Khola, a stream on the Nepal–China border. Some accounts say it blocked the river for a while before bursting through. The radar cannot settle that, but it shows the whole valley floor changed.

Sources
  • Radar: NISAR L2 GCOV, track 98 ascending, 19 and 31 Aug 2026.
  • ICIMOD; Kathmandu Post, 26 Aug 2026.

The warning minutes

By the reported times, the first SMS alerts went out 38 minutes after the collapse. The flood had already passed Rasuwagadhi.

  3. 9:05Hydrologists learn of the flood
  4. 9:15–9:16SMS alerts sent “to around 679,000 people in Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading and Chitwan”

  6. after 11:00Flood reaches Malekhu

Nepal time, as reported by ICIMOD. Some scientists doubt the arrival times, and one analysis puts the first collapse on 25 August.

How the flood compares with a normal period

The satellite passed ten times from June to October. Each bar is how much of the riverside changed between one pass and the next.

Details

The ice above the valley

Wet snow soaks up radar, so melting ice sends back a weaker echo. On the map, blue is ice whose echo is weaker than it was on 20 June. Drag the slider to move through the summer.

This may be a sign of melting. We could not check it on the ground, so we do not call it a measurement.

Details and sources
  • Fresh snow, bare ice and rock debris also change the echo, and NISAR's long wavelength reacts less to wet snow than shorter ones. Five months cannot show whether a glacier is shrinking.
  • Radar: NISAR L2 GCOV, track 98 ascending, ten dates from 20 Jun to 18 Oct 2026, averaged to about 50 m.
  • Glacial lakes: High Mountain Asia Near-Global Multi-Decadal Glacial Lake Inventory, version 1 (NASA NSIDC DAAC), period 2015 to 2018.
  • Heights: NASADEM, version 1 (NASA LP DAAC). Glacier outlines: OpenStreetMap contributors.

Where would water go?

Click anywhere on the map. A line shows the way downhill from that spot and which villages lie below it. Try one of the ringed glacial lakes.

The map only shows the shape of the land. Please do not read it as a flood forecast or an official hazard map.

Details and sources
  • The route follows the slope only. It does not know how much water there is, how deep it gets or how fast it moves.
  • Elevation: NASADEM Merged DEM Global 1 arc second, version 1 (NASA LP DAAC), measured in 2000, 30 m cells. Too coarse for narrow gorges.
  • Method: flow direction (D8) and height above the nearest drainage, pysheds library. See the data and methods page.

How we know it was the flood

  1. Seven ordinary 12-day periods on the same track show nothing like it.
  2. Four neighbouring rivers, measured the same way on the same dates, show no jump.
  3. Three satellite tracks saw the valley from different directions, and the change appears on all three.

Each dot is one 12-day period. The red dot is the one with the flood.

What it cannot tell you

  • The radar shows where the ground changed. It cannot show what people lost.
  • Anything else that happened in those 12 days is included.
  • Very steep slopes distort radar.
  • It looks back at an event and cannot be used as a warning system.
The measurement

NISAR is a radar satellite built by NASA and ISRO. It sends its own microwave pulses, so it sees through cloud and at night, and it passes over this valley on the same track every 12 days. For each pass we take the radar brightness of the ground (backscatter, HH and HV), average it over 5 × 5 pixels of 10 m to calm the grain, and divide the later pass by the earlier one. The result is in decibels: 3 dB is a doubling or halving.
