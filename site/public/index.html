How much riverside ground changed, from the source (left) to Betrawati (right)

Rasuwa, Nepal. 26 August 2026

The Rasuwa flood, traced from space by radar

On 26 August 2026 a flood came down the Lende Khola and the Bhote Koshi into Rasuwa. The radar satellite NISAR had passed over the valley a week earlier, and it passed again five days later. This site lays the two passes over each other and marks every place where the ground no longer looked the same.

  1. Follow the flood. Scroll this column. The map moves from the source to Betrawati, and each stop says how much of the riverside changed there.
  2. Compare it with a normal fortnight. Press . Left of the slider is an ordinary 12 days in the monsoon, right is the 12 days with the flood.
  3. Click any spot on the map. You get that spot's radar record for ten dates, as a chart and as a file you can download.
  4. Try the other tabs. plays June to October. shows the ice above the valley. draws where water runs from any point you click.

How to read the colours

The satellite passed over on 19 August and again on 31 August. The flood came on 26 August, in between. Where the ground reflected the radar very differently on the second pass, the map is coloured. Red means the echo got stronger, blue means it got weaker. Either colour means the surface changed. That can be new debris, boulders that moved, vegetation that is gone, or water where there was none.

The whole season, 12 days at a time

NISAR flew the same track over this valley ten times between 20 June and 18 October. The Season tab shows what changed between each pass and the next. Seven of the eight periods look much the same, a thin scatter of dots. One does not.

The ice above the valley

Liquid water in snow soaks up radar. When the surface of a glacier starts to melt, its echo drops. The Glaciers tab shows, for each date, where the echo was lower (blue) or higher (red) than on 20 June.

We have not checked this against weather or ground measurements. Fresh snow, bare ice and rock debris change the echo too, and NISAR's long radar wavelength reacts less to wet snow than shorter ones do. Read blue as “look here”, not as measured melt. Five months of data cannot show whether a glacier is shrinking.

Where water would go

Open the Water paths tab and click anywhere. The map draws the way downhill from that spot, one 30 m step at a time, using NASA's elevation model of the valley. Try a glacial lake or a side valley and see which villages lie below it.

The shaded strips are low ground: less than 10 m above the nearest river channel (purple) and 10 to 20 m above it (orange).

This is the shape of the land, not a flood forecast. It does not know how much water there is, how deep it gets or how fast it moves. The elevation data dates from the year 2000 and one cell is 30 m wide, which is too coarse for narrow gorges. It is not an official hazard map. For those, use the agencies listed at the end.

For students and researchers

Everything on this site is a measurement you can take away and check.

  1. Pick a place. Click it on the map. The card shows its radar brightness on all ten dates.
  2. Take the numbers. Press “Download CSV” on the card and plot them in a spreadsheet.
  3. Get the full tables. The downloads list has the river statistics for every 500 m and every period, the ten radar images, the glacier table and the lake outlines.
  4. Check the method. The data and methods page explains each step and how to run the scripts yourself.

Questions this data can answer

  • Which stretch of the river changed most, and had it changed back by October?
  • Do the side rivers show anything in the flood period?
  • At what height does the glacier echo drop first, and when does it recover?

How we know

The measurement

NISAR is a radar satellite built by NASA and ISRO. It sends its own microwave pulses, so it sees through cloud and at night, and it passes over this valley on the same track every 12 days. For each pass we take the radar brightness of the ground (backscatter, HH and HV polarisation), average it over 5 × 5 pixels of 10 m to calm the speckle, and divide the later pass by the earlier one. The result, in decibels, is what the map shows. Water, fresh debris, lost vegetation and moved boulders all change how the ground reflects radar.

Is it more than ordinary change?

Rivers in the monsoon change all the time. So we ran the same calculation for every other 12-day step on the same track from June to October 2026, and for neighbouring rivers that the published accounts do not place in the flood’s path. Each dot is one 12-day step. The red dot is the step that contains 26 August.

Three independent looks

Three NISAR tracks crossed the valley within nine days of the flood, each from a different direction. Switch between them under “More layers” on the map. A change that appears from all three directions is unlikely to be an artefact of one viewing angle.

Limits

  • A change in radar brightness is not a damage map. It shows where the surface changed, not what was lost.
  • The two passes are 12 days apart. Anything that happened in between is included, not only the flood.
  • The valleys are steep. On slopes facing the radar or hidden from it the signal is distorted; the product’s mask does not mark these places.
  • River lines come from OpenStreetMap and can sit tens of metres from the real channel.
  • The radar products are NISAR “provisional” releases: calibrated, not yet fully validated.
  • NISAR is not a warning system. It revisits every 12 days per track; this is a look back at what changed and where.
